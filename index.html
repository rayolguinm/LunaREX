The studio

LunaREX turns public planetary data into terrain people can measure, print and stand on.

Every scene starts as photons on an orbital detector and ends as a true-scale world in a headset, on a phone or on film. Between those ends sits engineering: calibration, stereo photogrammetry, mesh reconstruction and real-time rendering, each stage checked against the source data.

Mars comes from HiRISE, CTX and CaSSIS. The Moon comes from the public LROC archive. Europa comes from Galileo imagery and procedural terrain models built for roughness studies.

    Pixel

    Four measurements from the same grid

    Stereo turns the image pair into one elevation per post. Elevation is measured. Slope and shaded relief are derived from it. The thermal view is a physical model of how that relief redistributes sunlight, with cast shadows; it is checked against measured temperatures in the legend. The four cameras move together, so the maps stay registered.

    Instruments and missions

    HiRISE on Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter resolves Mars at about 0.3 m per pixel, the Context Camera gives the regional setting, CaSSIS on the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter adds color stereo and HRSC on Mars Express maps the planet in stereo. Lunar terrain here comes from the LROC cameras on Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter, and Europa from the Galileo Solid-State Imager. Europa Clipper carries the Europa Imaging System, the REASON ice-penetrating radar and the E-THEMIS thermal imager. Laser altimeters provide the control that stereo models are tied to: MOLA at Mars and LOLA at the Moon. Surface temperature is measured, not modeled, by THEMIS and TES at Mars and by Diviner at the Moon.

      Stereo photogrammetry and terrain models

      The HiRISE DTM method, and the expected vertical precision EP = ρ · GSD / (b/h), follow Kirk and colleagues, with DTM quality evaluated at the rover landing sites. HiRISE DTM production at the University of Arizona and its science uses are described by Sutton and colleagues; LROC NAC DTMs by Henriksen and colleagues. Other routes to the same product include the Ames Stereo Pipeline, large-scale cloud production and the HRSC global models. Lunar global terrain models set the context for NAC windows. The Europa DTMs in the lab are the USGS photogrammetrically controlled set and the Galileo stereo set. Two algorithms that underlie much of modern stereo are semi-global matching and bundle adjustment. Body radii and coordinate systems are the IAU values, within a planetary spatial data infrastructure.

        Slope, roughness and illumination

        Slope is computed with Horn's 3 by 3 finite-difference operator; other operators give measurably different slopes on the same grid. Slope and roughness depend on the baseline over which they are measured, so the lab always states its post spacing. Europa's slopes and roughness from Galileo stereo set the scale of the synthetic terrains, and meter-scale slopes from HiRISE DTMs are what landing-site selection relies on. The procedural roughness models use fractal subdivision and gradient noise. Cast shadows are found with a horizon test on the DTM, as in lunar polar illumination studies.

          Color, shading and photometry

          The classic rainbow color map hides and invents features because its lightness is not ordered; luminance is what carries form, and color maps differ measurably in how accurately they are read. The LunaREX altimetry ramp keeps the familiar hue order and makes lightness rise steadily. The Moon globe is lit with a Lommel-Seeliger law and the other two with Lambert shading, the two terms that planetary photometric functions combine; resolved photometric parameters of the Moon come from LROC WAC.

            Thermal model and its checks

            The thermal view is direct-beam radiative equilibrium on each facet, the slope term of rough-terrain thermal models, with the solar constant from the IAU nominal value and its measurement. It has no thermal inertia, which real surfaces do, so it is a ceiling on daytime temperature. The lunar parameters are those of the Diviner thermal models, and the model's 386 K ceiling sits at the 387 to 397 K equatorial maximum Diviner measures; sub-pixel roughness and permanent shadow matter at high incidence. For Mars the 301 K ceiling compares with a 290 K maximum mapped by Viking, and for Europa the 139 K ceiling with a 132 K daytime peak from Galileo; Europa's thermal properties and photometry are studied with Galileo, ALMA and Voyager data.

              The terrains themselves

              Europa's surface records an ocean beneath the ice: lenticulae, double ridges, chaos terrain, impact features such as Pwyll, possible plumes and the units of the geologic map. The Mars windows include layered sedimentary deposits, gullies, mid-latitude ground ice, recurring slope lineae, active dunes, brain terrain and the polar layered deposits. The lunar windows include irregular mare patches and Tycho.
